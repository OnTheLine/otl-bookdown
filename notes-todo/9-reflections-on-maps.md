Reflections on maps in email exchange with Amy Bloom, March 2026

Dear Amy

Thanks for reaching out again and apologies for my delay. I suppose I was trying to think of a rich response to your rich and thoughtful questions, but wasn't coming up clear responses right away. Your email today prompted me to try again:

> I recently finished reading More Than One Struggle, and I was particularly struck by your portrayal of school reform, housing policy, and civil rights activism as intertwined and at times competing spatial strategies. Your account makes clear how geography did not merely provide a backdrop, but actively structured both opportunity and constraint for reform efforts.

Thank you for your very close reading of the Milwaukee book, which I mostly wrote 25 years ago. While not trained as a geographer, I tend to be very attentive to place and space in my daily life, which seeps into my historical research and writing. During the two summers I spent interviewing activists around different parts of Milwaukee, I tried to envision how different neighborhoods -- where people had recently migrated, or sought to move into, or defended against outsiders -- also were central characters in the story. Also, as an historian of education, I was influenced by several authors who emphasized how communities often define themselves based on local institutions such as schools and their attendance boundaries.

> This leads me to a focused question. In your work on Milwaukee and Hartford, when reform movements confronted educational inequality, what ultimately proved more durable over time: formal policy changes, or transformations in spatial boundaries such as district lines and residential patterns? To what extent did long term outcomes hinge on legal frameworks versus the reshaping of demographic and physical space?

Great question, and I'm struggling to offer you a definitive answer, but perhaps there isn't one. Maybe my response is more philosophical than you expected, but it's the initial thought that came to my mind. Back in graduate school, two short but engaging essays that influenced my general thinking about how historians and social scientists explain cause and effect were:

- Carl Kaestle (one of my mentors), Theory in Educational History: A Middle Ground (in The City and Education in Four Nations, 1992) -- reading theories helps us to expand our analytical interpretations, but the best historical writing is not driven by one particular theory, since contexts greatly differ over time

- David Berliner, Educational Research: The Hardest Science of All (Educational Research 2002) -- while we call physics and chemistry the "hard sciences" those labels are more appropriate for the so-called "soft sciences" where shifting contexts and change over time prevents us from defining rigid laws of human behavior

So I'll try to ground the rest of my response in a specific context -- the metro Hartford CT region -- by articulating a question that's been on my mind over the years:

Q: Which explanation best explains racial change in Hartford's suburbs in the 1990s-2010s?

A) An economic explanation -- the early 1990s crash in housing prices, and the gradual expansion of the Black middle class, who became better positioned to purchase homes from elderly White families as they departed formerly all-White affordable neighborhoods

B) A political and legal explanation -- the 1989 multiracial coalition of civil rights activists who filed the Sheff v O'Neill school integration lawsuit in Connecticut state court (rather than federal court), built up a political movement to shift public opinion and won a split decision (4-3) at the CT supreme court in 1996, but White suburban politics in the state legislature (Republicans and Democrats combined) successfully blocked any meaningful implementation of integrated schools until 2003, when state leaders agreed to a modest voluntary integration program of city-suburban magnet schools and city-to-suburban transfers, without redrawing any city-suburban school district boundaries that the 1996 court labeled as unconstitutional

Clearly, I've framed that as a loaded question! Imagine we looked closely at 1990s-2010s census maps and school enrollment data to calculate the number of Black and Latino children moved into predominantly White neighborhoods and schools (call that A). Then imagine we look at magnet school and school transfer enrollment data and count the number of Black and Latino children who enrolled in newly-created integrated schools (call that B). I strongly suspect that A would be much larger than B, which initially sounds like housing economics are more influential that political and legal victories for civil rights.

But even if A is much larger than B, we need to understand that A did not happen in a vacuum. Black middle class migration into affordable suburban homes happened at the same time as a broader political and legal movement demanding the right to live and access better public education in suburban communities. In other words, as tempting as it is to treat A and B as separate explanations with separate data, I believe that the two were tightly intertwined. Your thoughts?

> I am also interested in your methodological perspective. Did working with digital mapping and spatial visualization influence how you interpreted these historical dynamics? In other words, did seeing these patterns mapped alter your sense of causation or continuity?

Let's try answering this one with a story that I hope will be insightful and also amusing. When I began graduate school in the early 1990s, I had to rely on paper census reports and paper maps (sometimes ones I colored by hand) to understand racial and economic change in neighborhoods over time. Advanced geographers were using early computer mapping, but that wasn't available to me. When I started to deliver presentations about my Milwaukee research (and subsequently my Hartford research) at historical conferences in the late 1990s and early 2000s, I realized that I was "talking with my hands" too much to the audiences, trying to "draw in the air" the changes in neighborhoods that I knew from the historical evidence.

I wasn't the only historian with this problem. I distinctly recall attending an Urban History Association meeting in Pittsburgh in the early 2000s, where I watched perhaps a dozen panel sessions, and as I recall telling others at the time, I saw a total of 2 maps that presenters handed out on paper while they spoke. Urban historians could "talk about" their evidence but most had no easy way to "show" it. Drove me a bit crazy, I must admit.

At the same time during the early-to-mid 2000s, two technology changes were influencing scholarly communication among historians and those in related fields. The first was PowerPoint, and I became an early adopter because I realized how to put not just words in front of audiences, but also visual images of evidence to make my argument more persuasive. Back in those days, my friends and I used a portable projector that I brought with me to conferences. The second tech change was easier software tools for mapping. A geography friend loaned me their expensive mapping software in the late 1990s, which I struggled to teach myself, and the tools got easier and workshops became more prevalent in the 2000s. Over time, I began "showing" my historical arguments and visual evidence to audiences through PowerPoint, then shareable Google Slides, different iterations of websites, and finally, audiences I do not meet in person (like you) through the open-access On The Line book with interactive maps. In fact, the Hands-On Data Visualization open-access book that I co-wrote with Ilya Ilyankou is my 2020 summary of everything I learned about mapping that I had not been able to do during the prior two decades.

Your question asks "...did seeing these patterns mapped alter your sense of causation or continuity?" Great question, but let's shift it from the passive to the active voice, perhaps like this: "Did your decades-long struggle to create and publicly share historical maps alter your sense of causation and continuity?" Absolutely yes! In fact, I think your question has helped me to realize, now as I'm retiring, how much my academic career can be summed up in a short sentence: He worked hard to get those damn pictures of historical change out of his head and onto the web to engage and educate the public.

thanks for nudging me to reflect and write -- and I hope my responses are helpful

Jack
